11.9 Germanium detector model (efficiency model “Ge detector” of the γ-spectrum window). Choose it as the third Detector efficiency model; the input boxes of the other two models are then replaced by the geometry boxes. It gives the peak (full-energy) efficiency εpeak, which scales the lines of the spectrum, and the total efficiency εtot, the probability that a γ deposits any energy in the crystal. Below the γ plot the window shows the two efficiency curves and a simulated Ge spectrum. The source is a point on the detector axis, at the distance z = source–endcap + endcap–crystal; the crystal is a cylinder of diameter D and length L (radius R = D/2).

  • Solid angle: Ω/4π = ½(1 − z/√(z² + R²)); photons are emitted at the polar angle θ, 0 ≤ θ ≤ atan(R/z), and enter the front face.
  • Path in the crystal: ℓ(θ) = L/cos θ if tan θ ≤ R/(z + L) (leaves through the back face), otherwise ℓ(θ) = R/sin θ − z/cos θ (leaves through the side). The dead layer td (Ge) is crossed first: the active path is ℓ′ = max(0, ℓ − td/cos θ).
  • Total efficiency: εtot = ½ ∫ sin θ · Twin · Tdead · [1 − e−μint ℓ′] dθ, with Twin = e−μAl tw/cos θ (Al window), Tdead = e−μtot td/cos θ and μint = μpe + μinc + μpair (photoelectric, incoherent, pair production). Coherent scattering is not counted as an interaction. The integral is evaluated numerically (160 angles).
  • Peak-to-total: εpeak = εtot · [μpe + μinc κ(E) + μpair κpp] / μint, where κ(E) is the probability that the Compton-scattered photon (energy ≈ E/2) ends up fully absorbed. It is computed recursively: κ(e) = [1 − e−μint(e) ℓeff] · [μpe + μinc κ(e/2) + …]/μint (up to five scattering generations), with the effective escape path ℓeff = 0.2·min(D, L). This factor is an empirical calibration (it makes a Ø60 × 60 mm crystal about 25 % relative efficiency, P/T ≈ 0.37 at 662 keV and 0.14 at 1332 keV), and κpp = κ(511 keV)² is the chance that both annihilation photons are absorbed. Photoabsorption always gives a full-energy count.
  • Scale factor: multiplies both efficiencies, to adjust the model to a calibration measurement. The info line under the boxes shows ε at 122, 662 and 1332.5 keV and the relative efficiency εpeak(1332.5 keV, 25 cm) / 1.2·10−3 (3″×3″ NaI at 25 cm), the usual way to rate a Ge detector.
  • Pile-up: with “from the model”, the number of pulses per full-energy count is the rate-weighted ratio εtot/εpeak of all γ lines in the slice, because every interaction produces a pulse, not only the full-energy ones. “Manual” keeps the value you type.
  • Plots: the efficiency curves extend to the upper energy of the γ spectrum (up to 10 MeV, the limit of the data). All three plots (γ spectrum, efficiency curves, simulated spectrum) can be zoomed with the mouse: drag a box (a narrow strip zooms one axis only). Every plot has its own “Reset zoom” button next to its Y-axis selector (and the double-click on the plot), which resets only that plot, and every plot is a collapsible block like the others. Every plot also has its legend (click to show or hide a curve, “Unselect all” / “Select all”) and its own linear or logarithmic Y axis.
  • Simulated Ge spectrum: every line gives a Gaussian full-energy peak (εpeak) and the rest, εtot − εpeak, is distributed as a single-Compton continuum (Klein–Nishina distribution of the electron energy up to the Compton edge) and a double-Compton part: the scattered photon E − T1 scatters a second time in the crystal (Klein–Nishina again, deposit T1 + T2) and then leaves, which fills the valley between the Compton edge and the peak. The share of the double part is p2(1 − κ)/[(1 − p2) + p2(1 − κ)], with p2 = [1 − e−μint(E/2) ℓeff] μinc/μint at E/2 (a rough estimate, applied to every line; the double-scattering shape is computed on a 1 % energy grid and stretched to the line energy; three or more scatterings are not simulated); above 1022 keV the pair-production escape gives single- and double-escape peaks at E − 511 and E − 1022 keV with the probabilities 2κ(1 − κ) and (1 − κ)², κ = κ(511 keV), taken from the pair fraction of εtot. Not included: backscatter peak, three or more scatterings, X-ray escape peaks, annihilation line from the surroundings, detector-edge effects.
  • Data: photon mass attenuation coefficients of Ge and Al, 28 energies between 10 keV and 10 MeV (log-log interpolation, K edge of Ge at 11.103 keV). The totals are the NIST XCOM/X-ray mass attenuation tables, the photoelectric, incoherent, coherent and pair components come from the xraylib (EPDL97) data up to 800 keV, and above that from the Klein–Nishina formula and the pair-production remainder; the high-energy photoelectric part and the Al components are scaled approximations (errors of some percent in ε above 1 MeV). Ge density 5.323 g/cm³, Al 2.699 g/cm³.
  • Limits: point source on the axis (extended sources and off-axis positions change ε a lot at short distances), no self-absorption in the sample, no absorbers between source and endcap, no coincidence summing (strong for cascades at short distances), no crystal hole or rounded edges. Expect εpeak within ~10–20 % of a calibrated detector at best (the peak-to-total part is a rough estimate); fit the scale factor to a measured point.

Figure: εpeak (full line) and εtot (dashed) of the default detector (Ø 60 × 60 mm, 30 mm from the crystal front, 0.7 mm dead layer, 1 mm Al window).

The model is first order: it is accurate for μ up to about 0.1; for larger values triple pulses, the dead time of the acquisition and the pulse shape (a pulse that is almost on top of another one is partly resolved) matter. It does not treat pile-up rejection circuits, pulse-height-dependent resolving times or the Compton continuum (only the peaks of the discrete lines are in S). Typical τ: 0.5 – 3 µs for a germanium detector with a Gaussian shaper, 0.1 – 0.5 µs for NaI/LaBr3 with fast shaping. To keep P below a few per cent, μ should stay below about 0.01 – 0.02, i.e. a pulse rate of 104 s−1 for τ = 1 µs.
